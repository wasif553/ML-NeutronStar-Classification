\begin{abstract}
Machine-learning classification of neutron-star composition is usually demonstrated on synthetic data in which every composition is a single equation of state (EoS) and test points are random draws along the same sequences.
We show first that the headline 97\% accuracy of a released benchmark of 770 such models is largely interpolation between neighbouring models (the MLP accuracy falls from 97\% to 94\% and 69\% when blocks of six and 24 models are withheld) and that its tabulated surface redshift leaks class information.
We then replace the benchmark by a population study of 360 EoS, 90 in each of four composition scenarios (nucleonic, hyperonic-like, strange, and neutron-decay dark-matter-admixed), solved with a full general-relativistic TOV, tidal and $f$-mode code that agrees with six cross-code reference $f$-mode frequencies (from an unpublished independent-code comparison) to 0.007\%.
With whole-EoS hold-out, six classifiers (logistic regression, $k$-NN, random forest, gradient boosting, SVM and an MLP ensemble) reach only 64\% accuracy even without measurement error, and a finite-reference kernel classifier performs similarly: within these populations the plateau appears to be set by the information in the observables, not by the learner.
Strange matter is separable (100\% recall when $f$ is measured accurately), hyperonic-like matter partly (74\%), but nucleonic and dark-matter-admixed matter are indistinguishable once the nuclear EoS is unknown (pairwise accuracy 52\%); with the nuclear EoS known exactly the dark component, which shifts $f$ by about 0.56\%, would need a $\sim$0.51\% $f$-mode measurement to be detected at 75\%.
With errors anchored on published measurements and forecasts (NICER-like 10\% in $M$ and 9\% in $R$, and a 20\% $f$-mode measurement) the accuracy is 34--37\% (chance 25\%) and conformal prediction sets contain 3.22 of four classes; 5\%, 5\% and 2\% errors would give 53\%, and the damping time adds no measurable classification gain in these tests.
Probabilities are well calibrated and conformal coverage is about 90\%.
Repeating the analysis on an independently constructed population (different parametrisations and priors) reproduces the nucleonic--dark-matter degeneracy (51\%) and the NICER-like accuracy (37\%), whereas the 64\% plateau and the identifiability of the hyperonic-like family depend on how that family is built.
This is a controlled forecasting and identifiability study, not an inference about observed stars. All conclusions remain conditional on the phenomenological EoS families, population priors, perturbative response and idealised error models.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Neutron stars probe matter at densities well above nuclear saturation \citep{LP04,OF16}, and a wide range of compositions---purely nucleonic matter, hyperon-rich matter, deconfined quark matter and neutron decay into a dark sector \citep{Husain22}---has been proposed.
Machine learning has been applied to neutron-star inference, for example to map observed masses and radii to the EoS \citep{Fujimoto18}. A natural question is how far a supervised classifier can distinguish composition from observables, and where the answer is limited by physics rather than by the algorithm.

A previous version of this work (arXiv:2512.23758v3) trained a small multilayer perceptron (MLP) on seven observables of 770 synthetic stellar models, each class being one EoS, and reported a test accuracy of 97.4\% on 116 held-out models. That number does not support the conclusions drawn from it, and a single sequence per class cannot say how well compositions can be told apart when the EoS itself is uncertain. This revision therefore does the following.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Audits the single-sequence benchmark} (Sec.~\ref{sec:legacy}): class sizes, mass coverage, a class-dependent redshift inconsistency, and the dependence of the accuracy on how test points are chosen.
\item \textbf{Builds a population of EoS} for each composition scenario and solves every one with a validated full-GR code (Sec.~\ref{sec:pop}); the validation against six independent $f$-mode frequencies is given in Table~\ref{tab:valid}.
\item \textbf{Evaluates with whole-EoS hold-out}, six learners and a kernel-Bayes reference classifier (Secs.~\ref{sec:methods}, \ref{sec:results}), under error scenarios taken from published measurements and forecasts as well as a scale that shows what precision would be needed.
\item \textbf{Tests statistical and EoS-family robustness}: EoS-level bootstrap intervals, learning curves, empirical prediction-set coverage, dark-fermion stability-window restrictions and out-of-family transfer between two parametrisations.
\end{enumerate}
Appendix~\ref{sec:legacynoise} keeps the single-sequence noise study, and Appendix~\ref{sec:changes} lists each claim of the earlier version that has been withdrawn or changed.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Class sizes.} The classes contain 222, 220, 122 and 206 models (not $\approx190$ each).
\item \emph{Sampling.} Models lie on a regular radius grid ($\Delta R=0.05$\,km) along each sequence, so neighbouring models are almost identical: for 96.5\% of the models the nearest neighbour in standardised $(\ln M,\ln R,\ln f,\ln\tau)$ belongs to the same class.
\item \emph{Mass coverage.} 49\% of the models have $M<0.5\,\Msun$ and 70\% have $M<1\,\Msun$, down to $7.2\times10^{-8}\,\Msun$.
\item \emph{The dark-matter sequence ends at $0.68\,\Msun$,} so at $M\gtrsim0.7\,\Msun$ the class is excluded by the mass alone.
\item \emph{Redshift inconsistency.} For the dark-matter and hyperonic classes the tabulated $z$ equals the Schwarzschild value implied by $(M,R)$ to $\approx$0.031\%; for the nucleonic and strange classes the median deviation is +18\% and +11\%. A logistic regression on the redshift residual alone separates \{nucleonic, strange\} from \{dark matter, hyperonic\} with 91\% cross-validated accuracy (57\% for $M$ alone, 72\% for $M$ and $R$). The redshift is therefore a class-dependent artefact and is not used in any analysis here.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Leakage from neighbouring models}\label{sec:leak}
Figure~\ref{fig:block} holds out contiguous blocks of increasing length (noise-free, 70/30 split, 15 repetitions). With all six observables ($M,R,f,Q,\tau,h_c$) the MLP accuracy decreases from 97\% (single-model holdout, equivalent to a random split) to 94\% at six-model blocks and 69\% at 24; the random forest drops from 99\% to 82\% and 42\%. With the four plausible observables $\ftau$ the MLP values are 78\%, 69\% and 45\%. A large part of the apparent skill is therefore interpolation between near-duplicate neighbours of the same sequence. $Q$ and $h_c$ are model-derived amplitudes rather than measurable quantities and are not used further.

Even with the leakage removed, a single sequence per class cannot say whether the \emph{composition} is identifiable when the EoS is uncertain: ``classifying the family'' and ``recognising one EoS'' are the same task. The remainder of the paper addresses this with populations.

\section{A population of equations of state}\label{sec:pop}
\subsection{Composition scenarios as EoS populations}
Each scenario is a family of EoS defined by a small number of parameters with a uniform prior (Table~\ref{tab:priors}); one draw from the prior is one EoS. The families are phenomenological parametrisations \emph{inspired by} the four scenarios of the earlier version, not microphysical calculations.

For the stable-dark-fermion benchmark, the relevant approximate mass interval is $937.9<m_\chi<938.783$\,MeV \citep{FG18}. The exploratory population-A prior extends to $939.2$\,MeV and is therefore \emph{not wholly physically admissible for that channel}. Out-of-window draws are treated solely as a sensitivity extension, not viable examples of the specified particle model. Section~\ref{sec:robust} reports the analysis restricted to the stability window and verifies which conclusions persist.
Draws are accepted only if they satisfy broad observational and consistency screens: $M_{\max}\ge1.97\,\Msun$, a causal speed of sound ($c_s^2\le1$) up to the centre of the maximum-mass star, $70\le\Lambda_{1.4}\le800$ and $10\le R_{1.4}\le14$\,km. The acceptance rates are 21\%, 57\%, 24\% and 26\% for the nucleonic, strange, dark-matter and hyperonic-like priors, so the screens remove most of the prior volume (mainly through $M_{\max}$ and causality, and for strange matter through $\Lambda_{1.4}$ and $R_{1.4}$). Observables are tabulated for stars of $1.2$--$1.9\,\Msun$ and evaluated on a mass grid over $1.2$--$1.85\,\Msun$, where all accepted EoS have stable stars.

The dark-matter-admixed scenario follows the effective-barotrope construction of the earlier work: baryons (nucleonic PP, $\mu_N=(\varepsilon+p)/n_B$) and a dark Fermi gas with $\mu_\chi=\sqrt{m_\chi^2+k_F^2}+\hbar cGn_\chi$ share one chemical potential, and the total pressure and energy density are summed at that potential. Because the dark component appears only above $\mu_N=m_\chi$, the stellar surface is purely baryonic and the dark component is at most a few per cent of the energy density even at the highest densities. The dark fermion is assigned baryon number $B_\chi=1$ in the baryon-conserving conversion benchmark. Writing $n_N=n_n+n_p$ for the nuclear baryon density, the conserved total baryon density is $n_{B,\mathrm{tot}}=n_N+n_\chi$; the quantity $n_B$ in $\mu_N=(\varepsilon_N+p_N)/n_B$ denotes the \emph{nuclear} density only. The conversion condition on the background is $\mu_N=\mu_\chi$; it does not itself determine the perturbative conversion timescale. By construction the DM scenario shares its nuclear prior with the nucleonic family. The classifier therefore tests whether the weak DM signature survives uncertainty in the baseline nuclear EoS.

\subsection{Solvers and validation}\label{sec:solver}
\paragraph{TOV and tidal deformability.} Stars are integrated with the Tolman--Oppenheimer--Volkoff equations \citep{Tolman39,OV39} and the tidal-deformability equation \citep{Hinderer08}.
\paragraph{Full-GR $f$-mode.} The $l=2$ quasi-normal $f$-mode of the non-rotating, barotropic star is computed from the linearised Einstein equations in the Regge--Wheeler gauge. The interior equations were derived symbolically (not taken from the literature) from the linearised Einstein equations and the conservation law, reduced to four first-order equations, and solved with regular boundary conditions at the centre and a vanishing Lagrangian pressure perturbation at the surface; this is the same physical system as the Lindblom--Detweiler formulation \citep{DL85}. The exterior solution is matched to the Zerilli equation \citep{Zerilli70}, and the complex eigenfrequency is the zero of the ingoing-wave amplitude; the real part gives $f$ and the imaginary part $\tau$. The exterior equations were checked against an independent symbolic derivation (residual $10^{-12}$). The complex eigenfrequency is found by a secant search on the analytic ingoing amplitude, starting within 3\% of the root.
\paragraph{Numerical error budget.} Varying one numerical setting at a time (interior and exterior integration tolerances from $10^{-8}$ to $10^{-13}$, the TOV tolerance, the surface cut-off, the starting radius at the centre and the outer matching radius from 1500 to 8000\,km) changes $f$ by at most $3\times10^{-8}$ in relative terms and $\tau$ by at most $5\times10^{-7}$, for SLy and for SLy with $G=100$\,fm$^2$ at $1.4\,\Msun$. These are more than five orders of magnitude smaller than the median dark-matter shift of interest (median 0.56\%). The remaining sensitivity is physical: changing the crust pressure scale by $\pm10\%$ shifts $f$ of the nuclear EoS by 0.20\% but changes the dark-matter shift only from 1.313\% to 1.338\% (SLy, $G=100$\,fm$^2$), so the \emph{shift} is robust to the crust treatment even though absolute frequencies are not.
\paragraph{Damping times.} Independent published damping times for stellar models were not available. The time dependence is $e^{i\omega t}$, so $\tau=1/\mathrm{Im}\,\omega$ and a damped mode has $\mathrm{Im}\,\omega>0$. The exterior machinery that produces $\mathrm{Im}\,\omega$ (Zerilli integration, projection onto asymptotic ingoing and outgoing solutions, complex root search) was benchmarked on a case with a published answer: with the horizon matched to purely ingoing waves it gives the fundamental $l=2$ quasi-normal mode of a Schwarzschild black hole, $M\omega=0.37366+0.08896\,i$, against $0.37367+0.08896\,i$ \citep{Leaver85}, i.e.\ relative errors of 3e-05 (real part) and 8e-06 (imaginary part). The interior contribution to $\tau$ is not independently tested, so statements about $\tau$ (in particular that it adds no classification information) are conditional on the solver.
\paragraph{Adiabatic response of the dark-matter configurations.} The perturbation equations use the equilibrium barotrope, i.e.\ chemical equilibrium is assumed to be maintained during the oscillation. As a bound on the opposite limit, $f$ was recomputed with a frozen-composition adiabatic response (baryon and dark numbers separately conserved, both components compressed together) for 24 dark-matter EoS at $1.4\,\Msun$: the median dark-matter shift is 0.64\% with the equilibrium response and 0.63\% with the frozen one, a difference of 1.1\% of the shift (at most 0.021\% of $f$). For the tested 24 configurations, the shift is insensitive at the reported level to the \emph{common-displacement} frozen-versus-equilibrium prescription. This is not a bound for genuine two-fluid oscillations: independent displacements, entrainment, chemical-relaxation timescales and nuclear composition gradients are not included. Hence the subpercent DM signature remains conditional on the chosen perturbative model.
\paragraph{Validation.} The crust is a single calibrated parameter (the absolute pressure scale of a four-piece SLy-like crust, 1.135 times the electron-gas value), fitted only to the radius and tidal deformability of six published EoS fits at $1.4\,\Msun$ (Table~\ref{tab:valid}); the radii are then reproduced to 0.0006\,km and $\Lambda$ to 0.05\%. The $f$-mode frequencies were \emph{not} used in any fit, and are reproduced to 0.007\% (maximum over the six EoS). The reference values are those of the finite-EoS pilot of the earlier work (\citealt{HusainPilot}), which used the separately developed RelModPy full-GR code (\url{https://github.com/fgittins/RelModPy}); the six values are listed in Table~\ref{tab:valid} so that the comparison can be repeated with any other code. The pilot manuscript is unpublished, so this is a comparison with an independent code rather than with published benchmark tables. In addition, the dark-matter frequency shift at fixed nuclear EoS ($\mathrm{SLy}$, $G=100$\,fm$^2$, $1.4\,\Msun$) is 25.7\,Hz against 25.8\,Hz in the reference, and a tabulated version of the same EoS agrees with the analytic one to 0.013\% in $f$. Damping times of $0.17$--$0.23$\,s are obtained for the six EoS, as expected for a $1.4\,\Msun$ $f$-mode; no independent reference values for $\tau$ were available.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}
\subsection{Whole-EoS validation}
Every EoS belongs to exactly one of five folds (stratified by scenario). In each of five rounds one fold is the test set, the next fold is the calibration set and the other three are the training set; no EoS appears in more than one role, so every reported number is for EoS the classifier has never seen. The partition is repeated for three random seeds; we report the mean over folds within a repetition and Student-$t$ 95\% intervals over the three repetitions. Observations are $(M,R,f,\tau)$ at masses drawn uniformly over $1.2$--$1.85\,\Msun$ (a mass prior shared by all classes), with independent noise draws, and classes are balanced (chance 25\%).

\subsection{Observational-error scenarios}\label{sec:errors}
Observables are perturbed multiplicatively, $x_j^{\rm obs}=x_j(1+\sigma_j\epsilon_j)$, $\epsilon_j\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ independent. Draws with a non-positive factor (only possible for the damping time at large $s$: 0.6\% of the draws at $s=2$ and 11\% at $s=4$; none elsewhere) are redrawn, so the noise is a truncated Gaussian; the kernel-Bayes likelihood ignores this truncation.
\emph{Literature-anchored scenarios:}
\textbf{NICER-like}: $\sigma_M=10\%$, $\sigma_R=9\%$, no $f$ or $\tau$; these are the 68\% uncertainties of the NICER analyses of PSR J0030+0451 ($M=1.34^{+0.15}_{-0.16}$ and $1.44^{+0.15}_{-0.14}\,\Msun$, $R=12.71^{+1.14}_{-1.19}$ and $13.02^{+1.24}_{-1.06}$\,km; \citealt{Riley19,Miller19}).
\textbf{NICER-like plus $f$-mode}: in addition $\sigma_f=20\%$, motivated by the forecast that third-generation detectors could measure the $f$-mode frequency of a GW170817-like inspiral to within a few hundred Hz \citep{Williams22}, i.e.\ roughly 10--25\% of $f$.
\emph{Precision scale:} to show what would be required, the same observables are also evaluated with errors $s\times(5\%,5\%,2\%,20\%)$ in $(M,R,f,\tau)$ for $s\in\{0.25,0.5,1,2,4\}$, and in the noise-free limit. These values are not forecasts for any instrument; they define a scale on which the literature-anchored scenarios can be placed. Three observable sets are used: $\{M,R\}$, $\{M,R,f\}$ and $\{M,R,f,\tau\}$.

\subsection{Classifiers}
Learners: logistic regression, $k$-nearest neighbours ($k=25$), a random forest (200 trees, minimum leaf 5), histogram gradient boosting, an RBF support-vector machine (8000 training samples) and an MLP deep ensemble \citep{Lakshminarayanan17} of three $2\times64$ ReLU networks (the architecture of the earlier version) on log-transformed, standardised inputs. Training data contain three noise realisations of 33 masses for each training EoS.

\subsection{Kernel-Bayes reference classifier}
For a reference set of $(\mathrm{EoS},M)$ points the posterior of class $k$ for an observation $\mathbf x^{\rm obs}$ is
\begin{equation}
P(k\mid\mathbf x^{\rm obs})\;\propto\;\frac1{N_k}\sum_{i\in k}\prod_j\mathcal N\!\left(x^{\rm obs}_j;\,x_{ij},\,(\sigma_jx_{ij})^2\right),
\end{equation}
which is the Bayes classifier \emph{for the finite discrete reference population under its nominal independent Gaussian error likelihood and prior}. At the largest error scales the simulated noise is positivity-truncated whereas the kernel expression is not, so the comparator is not exactly Bayes-optimal for that altered generating process. We use the training EoS as reference (the same information as the learners) and, separately, all EoS except the test fold (a larger reference). Unlike the single-sequence oracle of Appendix~\ref{sec:legacynoise}, this is not an upper bound, because the test EoS are not in the reference set; agreement across these methods suggests an information plateau for the sampled models but does not prove a universal upper bound.

\subsection{Uncertainty quantification}\label{sec:uncmethods}
\emph{Calibration}: expected calibration error (ECE, 15 bins), negative log-likelihood (NLL) and Brier score, before and after temperature scaling fitted on the calibration EoS. \emph{Epistemic uncertainty}: mutual information across ensemble members. \emph{Empirically calibrated prediction sets}: prediction sets are formed using the split-conformal score $1-\hat p(y\mid x)$ at target miscoverage $\alpha=0.1$ \citep{Vovk05,Angelopoulos23}, calibrated on the calibration EoS. The exchangeable unit is the EoS: the scores of all stars of a calibration EoS (33 masses, two noise draws each) are pooled, the correction uses $n$ equal to the number of calibration EoS (about 72), although scores from multiple stars per EoS are still pooled. This pooling does \emph{not} yield exchangeable calibration scores at the EoS level, and replacing the sample count by an EoS count in the quantile alone does not establish the finite-sample split-conformal guarantee. The 90\% target and observed coverage are therefore \emph{empirical held-out-EoS diagnostics}, not distribution-free guarantees. A formally grouped implementation would require prespecified EoS-level nonconformity scores and new calibration results. The observed coverage is marginal over stars from the sampled populations, not conditional on class or mass.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
\subsection{Noise-free separability of EoS populations}
Figure~\ref{fig:nf} shows the held-out-EoS accuracy without measurement error, and the left panel of Fig.~\ref{fig:cmpop} the confusion matrix. With $M$ and $R$ only the accuracy is 39\% (MLP ensemble; logistic regression 36\%, random forest 39\%); adding the $f$-mode raises it to 64\% and adding $\tau$ changes nothing (64\%). All learners agree within a few points. The confusion matrix shows why the ceiling is so low: strange matter is recognised perfectly (100\%), hyperonic-like matter in 74\% of cases, but nucleonic and dark-matter-admixed stars are assigned to each other almost at random (recall 44\% and 40\%; accuracy between the two classes 52\%, chance 50\%). Merging them into one class gives 84\% three-class accuracy; the baseline for this merged problem is 50\% (always predicting the merged class, which holds half of the stars), not the 33\% of three balanced classes. The reason is physical: the dark component shifts $f$ by only 0.56\% at fixed nuclear EoS (median over the population; 25.7\,Hz or 1.3\% for SLy with $G=100$\,fm$^2$; Sec.~\ref{sec:paired}), far less than the spread of $f$ at fixed mass produced by the uncertain nuclear EoS (about $\pm10\%$; Table~\ref{tab:popdata}).

\subsection{Errors anchored on published measurements}\label{sec:anchored}
Table~\ref{tab:scen} gives the accuracy under the literature-anchored scenarios and at selected points of the precision scale. With NICER-like errors ($M$ 10\%, $R$ 9\%) the accuracy is 34\% (MLP ensemble; kernel-Bayes 34\%), barely above the 25\% chance level; adding a 20\% $f$-mode measurement raises it to 37\%. The three-class accuracy after merging nucleonic and dark-matter-admixed matter is 51\%, no better than the 50\% majority-class baseline of the merged problem. \subsection{Dark matter when the nuclear EoS is known}\label{sec:paired}
To separate ``the dark component is small'' from ``the nuclear EoS is unknown'', the observables of each of the 90 dark-matter-admixed EoS were also computed for its own nuclear base EoS without the dark component, at the same masses. At fixed nuclear EoS and mass the dark component raises $f$ by a median of 0.56\% (5--95\% range 0.27--2.18\%; positive for 100\% of the pairs) and lowers $R$ by a median of -0.40\% (-1.46 to -0.20\%); the shift falls steeply with the self-interaction (Fig.~\ref{fig:paired}a; correlation of $\ln G$ with $\Delta f/f$ at $1.4\,\Msun$ is -0.95). If the observer were told the nuclear EoS exactly, the Bayes accuracy for dark matter versus no dark matter (equal priors, independent Gaussian errors, mass identical by construction) would reach 75\% at $\sigma_f=0.51\%$ when $R$ is known to 1\% (0.45\% for 5\%) and 90\% at $\sigma_f=0.20\%$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:paired}b); at the points of the precision scale it is 75\% at $s=0.25$ (0.5\% in $f$, 1.25\% in $R$), 58\% at $s=1$ and 52\% at $s=4$, and with a 20\% $f$-mode error and 9\% in $R$ it is 51\%. With the nuclear EoS \emph{unknown} the accuracy between the nucleonic and dark-matter classes is 52\% even without any measurement error (Sec.~\ref{sec:results}). The degeneracy therefore comes from the uncertainty of the nuclear EoS, not from the smallness of the shift alone: detecting the dark component needs both an $f$-mode measurement at the 0.5\% level and a nuclear EoS known to better than the shift itself.

\section{Discussion and limitations}
\paragraph{What the results support.} (i) A high accuracy on single-sequence data does not carry over to populations: with whole-EoS hold-out the highest accuracy reached by any method for these four scenarios is about 64\% even without measurement error (49\% in the alternative population B). (ii) The plateau looks informational: six different learners (including the MLP ensemble) and a kernel-Bayes classifier built on the exact error model agree, so within these priors a better learner is unlikely to help (the kernel-Bayes classifier uses a finite reference population and is not a rigorous upper bound). (iii) With the errors of current X-ray measurements and a plausible third-generation $f$-mode measurement, composition is essentially unidentifiable (34--37\% against 25\% chance); an accurate $f$-mode is the observable that would help, and the damping time adds nothing. (iv) Dark-matter-admixed matter is degenerate with nucleonic matter unless the nuclear EoS is known to better than the shift in $f$ that the dark component produces (median 0.56\%, up to about 2\% for weak self-interaction); with the nuclear EoS known exactly, an $f$-mode precision of about 0.51\% would give 75\% accuracy (Sec.~\ref{sec:paired}), in line with the EoS-conditional analysis of the earlier pilot. (v) Calibrated probabilities and empirical prediction sets describe the residual ambiguity, subject to the grouped-data calibration limitation.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\emph{Phenomenological families.} The EoS families are parametrisations with uniform priors, screened by $M_{\max}$, causality, $\Lambda_{1.4}$ and $R_{1.4}$; conclusions depend on these priors. The nucleonic--dark-matter degeneracy in particular follows from the dark-matter scenario sharing the nuclear prior. Microphysical models (for example a population of QMC EoS) would change the priors and might make some classes easier or harder to separate. The hyperonic-like family is a soft-then-stiff parametrisation, not a hyperonic calculation, and the strange family uses a constant speed of sound; the classifier therefore separates parametrisation families. A second population with different functional forms and priors (Sec.~\ref{sec:popB}) preserves the nucleonic--dark-matter degeneracy, the NICER-like accuracy and the recognition of strange matter (self-bound models with a surface density jump give a distinct $f$--$R$ relation in both), but not the 64\% plateau or the identifiability of the hyperonic-like family. No test with microphysically computed EoS (for example hyperonic or quark-matter calculations) has been made, and the conclusions about composition remain conditional on parametric families.
\emph{Dark matter.} The dark component is treated as an effective one-fluid barotrope in chemical equilibrium; the check against the two-fluid pilot is a single number ($25.7$ versus $25.8$\,Hz), and a frozen-composition response changes the shift by only 1.1\% (Sec.~\ref{sec:solver}). Independent displacement of the two fluids (a genuine two-fluid eigenmode calculation), other dark-sector models and other dark fractions are not explored.
\emph{Solver.} Non-rotating $l=2$ stars are considered. The main calculation assumes the equilibrium-barotrope response; the 24-EoS test varies that response under a shared displacement, not full two-fluid motion. Composition gradients, entrainment and crust elasticity are omitted. Black-hole QNM benchmarks test the exterior complex-frequency machinery, but independently published stellar damping-time benchmarks are still unavailable.
\emph{Errors.} Independent multiplicative noise is used, with resampling of nonpositive factors at high errors while the kernel reference uses the untruncated Gaussian likelihood. NICER and $f$-mode uncertainties are motivated by individual-source measurements and forecasts, not a joint detected sample. Correlated errors, selection effects and systematic theoretical uncertainty are unmodelled.
\emph{Statistics and sampling.} 90 EoS per scenario (48 in population B) and three fold partitions that reuse the same EoS, so the $t$-intervals reflect partition and noise variability only; EoS-level bootstrap intervals and learning curves are given in Sec.~\ref{sec:robust}. A uniform mass prior over $1.2$--$1.85\,\Msun$ is used; conformal prediction sets are empirically calibrated and their coverage is marginal only.
\emph{Observational realism.} The errors are independent and Gaussian; real NICER posteriors are correlated and non-Gaussian, and the GW170817 and NICER posteriors themselves were not used.
\emph{Not comparable to earlier data.} The new data come from a new solver and cannot be compared point by point with the single-sequence data set.

\section{Conclusions}
Evaluated on populations of EoS with whole-EoS hold-out, machine-learning classification of neutron-star composition is far less decisive than the 97\% single-sequence benchmark suggested: the accuracy plateau is about 64\% even without measurement error (49\% in an alternative population with different parametrisations), it appears to be set by the information in the observables rather than by the learner, strange matter is the only scenario that is cleanly separable (in the family defined here), and nucleonic and dark-matter-admixed matter are not distinguishable when the nuclear EoS is unknown. With errors anchored on current X-ray measurements and a forecast $f$-mode precision the accuracy is 34--37\%, and calibrated prediction sets contain about 3.22 of four classes.

\section{Single-sequence noise study (supplement)}\label{sec:legacynoise}
This appendix keeps the noise, oracle and uncertainty analysis performed on the single-sequence benchmark, with blocked validation. It is superseded by the population study for questions about composition, but it isolates the effect of measurement error on the original data and provides an exact oracle bound.

\subsection*{Mass regimes, errors, learners, oracle}
Three mass regimes are used: \textbf{A}, all models ($N=770$); \textbf{B}, $M\ge0.5\,\Msun$ ($N=395$, with 40 dark-matter models); \textbf{C}, $M\ge1\,\Msun$ ($N=232$, three classes). Contiguous mass blocks of six models are assigned at random to training (60\%), calibration (20\%) and test (20\%) roles; the analysis is repeated for 10 random assignments. Observables are perturbed as $x_j(1+s\sigma_j\epsilon_j)$ with $(\sigma_M,\sigma_R,\sigma_f,\sigma_\tau)=(5,5,2,20)\%$ at $s=1$. The learner is a three-member MLP ensemble, with logistic regression and a random forest as baselines. The oracle Bayes classifier knows the exact sequences (including the test blocks) and the error model,
\begin{equation}
P(k\mid\mathbf x^{\rm obs})\;\propto\;\frac1{N_k}\sum_{i\in k}\;\prod_j \mathcal N\!\left(x^{\rm obs}_j;\,x_{ij},\,(s\sigma_j x_{ij})^2\right),
\end{equation}
and is an upper bound for learners trained on finite data.

\subsection*{Results}
At $s=1$ in regime B the ensemble reaches 47\% with $\ftau$ against an oracle ceiling of 65\% (chance 25\%); $M$ and $R$ alone give 29\% (oracle 49\%). In regime C (three classes) the values are 50\% (ensemble) and 65\% (oracle). Adding $f$ raises the oracle from 49\% to 64\%; adding a 20\% $\tau$ gives 65\%. Strange and hyperonic matter are the most confused pair (36\% of strange models labelled hyperonic and 37\% the reverse). The raw ensemble is overconfident (ECE 0.188, NLL 1.13 at $s=1$, regime B; oracle 0.040, 0.74); temperature scaling reduces them to 0.096 and 1.00. Conformal sets have about 90\% marginal coverage and contain 2.35 of four classes at $s=1$ (oracle 1.85). When the heaviest 25\% of each class is withheld, the ensemble reaches 50\% (oracle 80\%) and the conformal sets cover only 0.81, since exchangeability fails; the ensemble's mutual information rises from 0.010 to 0.025 nats, an incomplete warning.
